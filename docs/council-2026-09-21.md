# Council, 2026-09-21

Six personas (AI agents, each given only the fact brief below) argued FreeSeries against Sonar, Equalizer APO + Peace and Arctis Sound Manager. Kept as the record of what has to be true before going public. The personas' technical claims are arguments, not measurements.

# The Pitcher's opening

FreeSeries is for the Windows gamer who already owns Dolby Atmos or DTS Headphone:X and wants a parametric EQ after it, plus Game, Media and Chat channels, without GG or an account; nothing else on Windows gives you that order of operations.

Sonar has mic noise suppression, a large preset library and ChatMix, and I have none of those. But the moment you want its EQ, its devices replace your spatial setup and you get Sonar's own spatial audio, which many people dislike, inside a heavy suite with an account and telemetry. I keep your renderer and EQ what comes out of it.

Equalizer APO with Peace beats me on latency: effectively zero against my 24 milliseconds. But an APO sits before the spatial renderer and you cannot reorder it, so it cannot EQ the binaural output. It has no mixer, and its setup, with device selection, a reboot and breakage on driver updates, loses the casual player in this room.

Arctis Sound Manager is Linux only, so it takes no user from me. It is a warning. In six months it shipped 312 game presets, mic processing, device control, clip capture, translations and an update check. I have six presets, four of which nobody has listened to, one user, and no repository.

First weakness, trust: an unsigned installer that asks for UAC to install someone else's driver, no updater, no README, and a crash leaves the PC silent until you change the Windows default. I would publish the source, document what the installer touches, hand the default device back on crash, and get code signing before asking strangers to run it. Second, latency and capture glitches under CPU load, which disqualifies me for competitive play. The IAudioClient3 backend targeting about 10 milliseconds is planned, not built; it goes ahead of every new feature. I have also never switched the headset off to test failover or pressed a hotkey, and I will before launch.

What I want from the room: tell me the one thing that would stop you installing it, so I fix that before going public.

# The room answers the pitch

The one thing that stops me installing it is the mic. There's no noise suppression, so my keyboard goes straight into Discord and my team mutes me. Nothing else matters until that's fixed, and you said the signed driver can't even give you a capture device. My answer today is no.

## The audio engineer (Equalizer APO + Peace)
I'll concede the ordering point. An APO runs before the endpoint's spatial renderer and I cannot move it, so my AutoEq correction is applied to the channel bed, not to the binaural signal that reaches my ears. Correcting the headphone after the renderer is the correct place for it, and Equalizer APO cannot do that. Your +6.0 dB in, +6.0 dB out reading is also the kind of evidence I trust.

My first objection is about clocks. You capture from one device and render to another, and those are two clocks. From general audio engineering knowledge, they drift, and the brief mentions no drift compensation or resampler. A 24-second soak cannot show this. Over two hours a queue with no correction either underruns or grows, and your 24 ms figure stops being true. The 13 to 31 ms spread between runs already suggests the buffer fill is not under control. I'd also want to know what happens when the virtual device and the headset run at different sample rates.

My second objection is about gain staging. The loopback signal comes after the renderer and after the mixer, so it may already be near full scale. You then add AutoEq boosts and a "Footsteps" preset on top, and a soft limiter catches the overs. A limiter acting on binaural content changes level in exactly the high-frequency bands that carry the localisation cues. Broad tonal EQ after the renderer is fine. Narrow boosts in the 4 to 10 kHz region will fight the HRTF. That makes presets nobody listened to worse than useless here. The agent is the same problem: it sees the programme spectrum, not the headphone's response, so it has no target to tune towards.

The thing that would stop me installing it is the crash case that leaves the PC silent until I change the Windows default. It would happen mid-session, on a driver you can't patch.

Before I recommend it to anyone, I'd want these published: a four-hour loopback run with latency logged continuously to show drift, the dropout count under CPU load, a null test with the EQ bypassed to show the path is bit-transparent, and THD+N with the limiter engaged.

## The Arctis Sound Manager contributor (Linux)
I envy one thing: you keep the user's own Atmos or DTS renderer in the chain and EQ what comes out of it. We can't; HeSuVi virtual 7.1 is our ceiling. And you measured it: +6.0 dB in, +6.0 dB out.

Copy three things first. One-click bug reports: you have one user on one Nova Pro Wireless, and every stranger's machine is one you can't reproduce, so the report must carry devices, driver state and log. The update check: without it, bugs you've fixed live on in other people's installs. And safe mode after a crash plus a startup self-check: if the last run died, come up with processing off and the Windows default handed back, and confirm the fxvad driver is there before routing anything into it.

Don't chase our 312 presets. It's the number people quote, but you have six and nobody has listened to four. Listen to those; your Equalizer APO import covers the rest. Translations and a preset site follow users, they don't create them.

What would stop me installing: no source. An unsigned installer asking for UAC to install someone else's driver, with no repository to read, is a no. Publish first, sign second. The silent PC after a crash is what would make me uninstall.

The hard-won lesson: once you're public, your hardware stops being the hardware. Your failover reads the Nova Pro's HID status and you've never switched the headset off. Most reports will come from devices you don't own, so test the generic path hardest.

## The security and release engineer
You overrate the borrowed driver. A Microsoft-signed fxvad.sys, verified before install, is safer than kernel code you wrote in two days. Pin its hash and say where the binary comes from. You underrate the missing updater: you named it once and left it off your fix list. You are one maintainer, on an undocumented interface a Windows update can change, with a driver you cannot patch. Without an update channel, every install stays frozen on the broken version. And "hand the default back on crash" cannot be done by the process that crashed. From general experience, that needs a watchdog or a restore at next logon.

Before public release, in order: crash recovery that survives the app dying; public source with a README listing everything the installer touches; a tested uninstall that removes the driver instances and the logon task; an updater that verifies signatures against a key kept off the build machine; then code signing. Signing comes last because, in my experience, a new certificate still trips SmartScreen until it earns reputation.

What would stop me installing: an elevating binary I cannot tie to source. Build releases in public CI from a tagged commit and publish the hashes.

The agent endpoint is an asset. Loopback bind, bearer token, Origin and Host checks, off by default: that is handled properly, and its scope is narrower than the installer's. Its worst case is gain. An agent that has read a poisoned page can push boost into someone's ears, so clamp agent-set gain server-side and keep the limiter on. It becomes a liability the day it can touch routing or the default device.

# Fact brief for the council (everything here is true as of 2026-09-21; do not invent beyond it)

## FreeSeries (the project being judged)
Free, open-source (AGPL-3.0) alternative to SteelSeries Sonar for Windows 10/11. Version 0.3.0, built by one developer with an AI pair over about two days. Not public yet: no repository, no releases, no code signing, no in-app updater, no README. One user so far (the developer), on an Arctis Nova Pro Wireless.

How it works: apps play into a virtual audio device (FxSound's Microsoft-signed `fxvad.sys` driver, installed by the app behind one UAC prompt; FreeSeries did not write or sign a driver). The user turns on Windows spatial sound (Dolby Atmos / DTS Headphone:X / Windows Sonic, whichever they own) on that virtual device. A Rust engine captures the device by WASAPI loopback, which already contains the spatial renderer's binaural output, applies EQ, and plays to the real headphones. So: the user's own Atmos/DTS plus a parametric EQ after it, which Windows cannot otherwise do and which is the whole reason the project exists (Sonar forces its own spatial audio, widely disliked, if you want its EQ).

Measured on hardware: a +6.0 dB test band reads +6.0 dB at the output; latency was 64 ms, now about 24 ms (13 to 31 ms per run through the mixer) after queue trimming; 24 s two-channel soak with 0 dropouts. Known: capture glitches logged under heavy CPU load (during compiler runs); fix planned (lock-free ring, MMCSS priority, or an IAudioClient3 low-latency backend targeting ~10 ms), not built.

Features built:
- Parametric EQ editor (unlimited-ish bands, peak/shelves, drag on a graph, live spectrum analyser, A/B bypass, preamp with headroom warning, soft limiter).
- Channels: Game, Media, Chat, each a second/third instance of the same signed driver, each with its own EQ, fader, mute, live peak meter; a Game/Media balance crossfader. Mixing-console UI. Apps are dragged onto a channel; this drives the per-app output setting of Windows through an undocumented interface (`AudioPolicyConfig`, the same one EarTrumpet and SoundSwitch use).
- Headphone correction per output device from the AutoEq database (thousands of measured headphones), separate from the per-channel "sound" preset.
- 6 built-in sound presets (Footsteps, Bass boost, Voice clarity, Late night, two ported from the dev's own setup). Tuned by calculation; nobody has listened to four of them. Import/export in Equalizer APO / AutoEq text format. No Sonar preset import.
- Output priority list with automatic failover: headset off -> monitor speakers, headset back on -> back (uses the Arctis Nova Pro's HID status; generic devices fall back to presence). The failover has not been tested by actually switching the headset off.
- Tray app, start with Windows via a logon task, global hotkeys (never actually pressed in testing), setup wizard, error log, dark Apple-style UI (Tauri + React).
- Agent access: a local MCP endpoint (127.0.0.1, bearer token, off by default) so an AI agent such as Claude can read the setup, measure the spectrum of what is playing, set a channel's EQ, and apply a headphone correction. Verified end to end with curl. The agent cannot hear; it sees the content's spectrum, not the headphone's response.
- On quit, apps pinned to a channel are handed back to the Windows default so they don't go silent.

Not built: microphone processing of any kind (noise suppression, mic EQ; needs a virtual capture device the signed driver doesn't offer), game-launch auto-switching of presets, device controls (battery, sidetone, ANC, OLED, ChatMix dial), a preset community, translations, Linux/macOS.

Risks the developer already knows: depends on a third party's signed driver (FxSound) it cannot fix or re-sign; per-app routing rests on an undocumented Windows interface; unsigned installer will trip SmartScreen; every sample takes an extra loopback hop (latency above); if the app crashes while it is the default device, the PC is silent until the user switches the Windows default back.

## SteelSeries Sonar (in SteelSeries GG)
Free, closed source, Windows only, requires the GG suite and (for full features) an account. Game/Chat/Media/Aux/Mic virtual devices with its own signed drivers, per-channel parametric EQ, large library of game presets, ChatMix, mic noise suppression (ClearCast AI), its own spatial audio. Works with any headset. Common complaints: GG is heavy, account and telemetry, its spatial audio quality, and that using Sonar's EQ means Sonar's devices replace your Atmos/DTS setup.

## Equalizer APO + Peace
Free, open source, Windows. System-wide EQ as an Audio Processing Object inside the Windows audio engine: effectively zero added latency, very mature, AutoEq-friendly. No channels/mixer, setup is intimidating for non-technical users (device selection, reboot, breaks on some driver updates), and an APO sits before the endpoint's spatial renderer can't be reordered by the user.

## Arctis Sound Manager (github.com/loteran/Arctis-Sound-Manager)
Linux only, Python, GPL-3.0, built on PipeWire. Created 2026-03-09, 133 stars, 15 forks, active (pushed 2026-09-20), Discord, Crowdin translations, community preset and theme sites, packaged for pacman/AUR/COPR/PPA/PyPI, in-app update check and one-click bug reports. Features: 4-channel mixer (Game/Chat/Media/Output) with automatic media routing, Sonar-style EQ with 312 game presets + chat + mic presets, macro Bass/Voice/Treble sliders, HeSuVi virtual 7.1, volume boost, smart volume compressor, hardware EQ on nine models, mic processing (rnnoise / DeepFilterNet, gate, compressor), audio profiles, 30-second clip capture with one track per channel, Stream Guard for Discord screen shares, device control (ANC, battery, sidetone, Nova Pro OLED), runs without a SteelSeries headset for the audio parts.

## Others worth a mention
FxSound (free, the driver FreeSeries borrows; simple EQ/effects, no channels), Voicemeeter (powerful virtual mixer, steep learning curve, donationware), EarTrumpet (per-app volume and device routing only, no EQ), Dolby Access / DTS Sound Unbound (paid spatial audio, the thing FreeSeries lets you keep).
